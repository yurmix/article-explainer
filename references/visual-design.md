# Visual direction and review

## Choose a direction before expanding

Honor supplied branding and references. Otherwise choose a considered direction that fits the subject and audience without asking the user to design it. Record a short working brief: intended mood, typography hierarchy, restrained palette, spacing/composition, illustration language, and how motion teaches. Make concrete choices rather than describing the result as "modern" or "polished." No mandatory font, palette, template, or paid asset is required.

Build representative opening, explanation, and conclusion scenes first. Inspect them in the actual renderer before applying the system throughout. Reuse typography, color meanings, alignment, and controls; vary composition when the teaching purpose changes.

## Focus the viewing experience

The active explanation should be the visual focal point. Keep the heading, stage, captions, and compact player coherent. Avoid stacking large mastheads, competing cards, repeated labels, and equal-weight panels around them. The viewer should immediately recognize what to watch and how to play or pause.

Keep chat useful and reachable without letting it crowd the stage. On narrow screens, use intentional stacking, a drawer, or a separate view. Do not merely shrink desktop diagrams or move a long reference panel beneath them unchanged.

Use progressive disclosure for references, transcript, editorial decisions, and production details. Show a readable citation such as a section title or "View supporting passage" near a claim; expose full provenance on demand. Preserve stable passage IDs in the content model and review material, but do not fill the default viewer with internal IDs or exhaustive citation chips. Evidence must remain easy to reach.

Keep script approval and production diagnostics in a review view or companion document. Show essential missing-feature status clearly and briefly near the relevant control; do not hide missing audio/chat or suggest that a preview is complete. A silent review build should still demonstrate the intended final visual experience.

## Make scenes explain

Choose a visual form for the idea: a comparison, boundary, sequence, feedback loop, causal relationship, annotated source image, or concrete illustrative situation. Rectangles and arrows are useful when the relationship calls for them; repeating the same generic flowchart for every scene is not visual storytelling.

Use position, scale, emphasis, and purposeful reveals to direct attention. A change on screen should explain a change in the idea. Give labels breathing room and hold important states long enough to read. Avoid decorative motion, gratuitous gradients, or generated imagery that adds no understanding. Preserve literal accuracy and label illustrative examples. Do not invent quantities to make a chart.

## Review rendered evidence

Inspect screenshots or frames at representative desktop and phone sizes, plus meaningful playback states. Check the initial viewport and full-page composition as well as individual scenes. Review at the size the audience will actually see.

- **Hierarchy:** Is the teaching point unmistakable? Do navigation, citations, or status text compete with it?
- **Composition:** Are spacing, alignment, scale, and density intentional? Are boxes and borders organizing content or merely multiplying clutter?
- **Typography and color:** Are type roles consistent, line lengths comfortable, and labels/captions legible? Does color have a stable meaning with accessible contrast?
- **Explanatory value:** Does the visual reveal something beyond the spoken sentence? Are scene variations purposeful and transitions coherent?
- **Responsive and motion states:** Do diagrams recompose legibly, controls stay usable, and reduced-motion views retain the explanation?

Identify visible defects, fix them, and recheck the affected states. This review is autonomous unless the user requested design approval. Record what was visually inspected separately from functional checks. If rendering or visual inspection is unavailable, disclose that limitation rather than claiming a polished result. Payment is not a substitute for this review.
